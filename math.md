* **Total Assets:** Everything the company owns that has financial value. This includes tangible items (cash, inventory, property, factories, equipment) and intangible items (patents, trademarks).
* **Total Liabilities:** Everything the company owes to others. This includes bank debt, accounts payable to suppliers, mortgages, and taxes owed.

You can find both of these numbers on a company's **Balance Sheet**.

### Why Does Book Value Matter?

Investors primarily use book value to figure out if a company's stock is overvalued or undervalued by comparing it to the company's **Market Value** (also known as Market Capitalization, which is the total value of all its shares combined).

This comparison is often expressed as the **Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio**.

* **Market Value > Book Value (P/B greater than 1):** This is the most common scenario for healthy companies. It means investors believe the company will generate future profits and grow, so they are willing to pay a premium above the raw value of the company's physical assets.
* **Market Value < Book Value (P/B less than 1):** This means the stock market values the company at *less* than the sum of its parts. This usually signals one of two things: the stock is a hidden bargain (undervalued), or the company is in serious financial trouble and investors believe its assets are actually worth less than what is recorded on the balance sheet.



### A Quick Example

Imagine a fictional manufacturing company, *Alpha Widgets*:

* Alpha Widgets owns a factory, machinery, and cash totaling **$10 million** (Total Assets).
* They owe the bank **$4 million** in loans (Total Liabilities).

The Book Value of Alpha Widgets is **$6 million**.

If Alpha Widgets has 1 million shares of stock outstanding, the **Book Value Per Share (BVPS)** would be **$6.00**. If the stock is currently trading at $5.00 a share on the stock market, a value investor might take a very close look at buying it, since they are essentially buying $6.00 worth of assets for five bucks.
