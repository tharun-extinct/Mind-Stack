While the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model is considered the gold standard for finding intrinsic value, it relies heavily on predicting the future—which is notoriously difficult. Because of this, investors have developed several other models to calculate intrinsic value, each suited to different types of companies.

Here are the most widely used alternative models:

### **1. The Dividend Discount Model (DDM)**

The DDM is essentially a specialized version of the DCF, but instead of projecting free cash flows, it projects the actual cash returned to shareholders: dividends. This model assumes a company is worth the sum of all its future dividend payments, discounted back to their present value.

* **Best for:** Mature, stable companies with a long, consistent history of paying out and growing dividends (e.g., Coca-Cola, Johnson & Johnson).
* **The Math (Gordon Growth Model):**

$$Intrinsic\ Value = \frac{D_1}{r - g}$$



*(Where $D_1$ is next year's expected dividend, $r$ is the discount rate, and $g$ is the expected constant growth rate of the dividend).*

### **2. Benjamin Graham’s Formula (The Graham Number)**

Benjamin Graham, known as the "father of value investing" and Warren Buffett's mentor, wanted a quick, conservative way to find the absolute maximum price a defensive investor should pay for a stock. This model combines earnings and book value.

* **Best for:** Finding deep value stocks and avoiding overpaying for hype. It works best for older, asset-heavy businesses (manufacturing, industrials, banks).
* **The Math:** Graham believed a P/E ratio shouldn't exceed 15, and a P/B ratio shouldn't exceed 1.5 ($15 \times 1.5 = 22.5$).

$$Graham\ Number = \sqrt{22.5 \times EPS \times BVPS}$$



*(Where $EPS$ is Earnings Per Share and $BVPS$ is Book Value Per Share).*

### **3. Earnings Power Value (EPV)**

Developed by Columbia University professor Bruce Greenwald, EPV is a fantastic alternative to the DCF because it completely **ignores future growth**. Growth is the hardest thing to predict and the easiest thing for analysts to get wrong. EPV calculates what the company is worth right now, assuming its current earnings stay flat forever.

* **Best for:** Established companies in stable industries. If the calculated EPV is higher than the stock price, you are essentially getting any future growth for free.
* **The Math:** 
$$EPV = \frac{Adjusted\ Earnings}{Cost\ of\ Capital}$$



### **4. Asset-Based Valuation (Net Asset Value)**

This loops right back to our discussion on Book Value. An asset-based approach calculates a company's intrinsic value by adding up what it owns and subtracting what it owes. However, instead of using the historical accounting numbers (Book Value), an analyst adjusts the assets to their actual, current market prices (Fair Market Value).

* **Best for:** Real estate holding companies, investment funds, and companies facing liquidation or bankruptcy.
* **The Math:**

$$Intrinsic\ Value = Fair\ Market\ Value\ of\ Assets - Total\ Liabilities$$



### **5. Relative Valuation (Multiples)**

While purists argue this is *relative* value rather than *intrinsic* value, it is the most common valuation method used on Wall Street. Instead of calculating value in a vacuum, you figure out what a company is worth by comparing it to similar companies.

* **Best for:** Quick comparisons, IPOs, and tech companies with unpredictable future cash flows.
* **The Math:** You find the average valuation multiple (like the Price-to-Earnings or P/E ratio) for the industry. If the industry average P/E is 20, and your company has an EPS of $3, the implied value is $60.

$$Implied\ Value = Industry\ Average\ Multiple \times Company's\ Metric$$



**Which one should you use?**
Professional investors rarely rely on just one. They usually run two or three of these models on the same stock to create a "range of values." If a stock is trading at $40, and the DCF says it's worth $65, the Graham Number says $55, and the EPV says $50, the investor has a high degree of confidence that the stock is intrinsically undervalued.
